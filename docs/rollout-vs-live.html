Live solver, rollouts and MCTS

Three ways to choose a move in doubles, built on the same engine and the same one-turn table, and how they compare. How the engine works.

Short version. On 4 positions all three game-playing methods mostly agree with the live solver's top pick (2–3 of 4) and give up about 0.06–0.08 of guaranteed value by its own table. Reruns of the same method disagree about as often, so most of the difference is noise. Six-game matches show nothing.
The three methods
MethodHow it fills the tableAnswer is
Live solver (search.solve_budget)Resolves every pair of actions under every chance outcome, then does it again one turn deeper. Exact probabilities, but it can only afford a few options per side.The mix that guarantees the most, from the solved matrix.
Rollouts (rollout.solve_rollout)For each pair of actions, plays the first turn and then random (or greedy) games to the end. A cell is the mean result.The solved matrix of those means.
MCTS (mcts.solve_mcts)Grows a tree: each node has the same kind of table, each side picks by regret matching, and playouts score new nodes. The first turn's table is the mean over all iterations.The average strategy the two sides played at the root.
Same table, three ways

All three use prior.candidate_joint_actions at the root: the top two damaging moves and the best switch per slot, no status moves (the live setting's root filter). So they fill an identical table of rows × columns, and a difference in the answer comes from how the cells are valued, not from which actions were considered.

Audit: do they agree with the live solver?

Where they disagree

Position 2: MCTS with greedy playouts prefers Venusaur earthquake to the live solver's sludgebomb->Dragonite, and its own rerun agrees with neither. Position 3: rollouts and both MCTS runs switch Salamence to Gengar where the live solver attacks Garchomp, by a gap of about 0.06–0.10. These are the cases where playing games to the end sees something a 2-turn score does not, or where noise decides.

Head to head

6 games per pairing, 2 s per move on one core, 4-mon teams drawn from the same 8-mon pool, sides swapped on each team pair, games capped at 40 turns. Each bot samples its move from its mix.

What to take from it
  • The live solver is exact on the options it considers, but a deeper search costs seconds on every core.
  • Playing games sees whole games, not two turns, so it can value things the 2-turn score misses. Its noise is large at a few seconds, and a rerun often picks something else.
  • MCTS spends its games where they matter, which makes its pick steadier than plain rollouts on the same budget.
  • None of these results is a winner: the match counts are far too small to rank the methods.
